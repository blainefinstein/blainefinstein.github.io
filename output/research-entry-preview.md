### Politics Without a Price: External service provision and democratic accountability

Shows how external service provision can weaken democratic accountability: experimental evidence from the Philippines links a community-driven development program to reduced public service spending and procurement directed toward political supporters, with effects concentrated where electoral competition is weak.

<details>

<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>

Democratic accountability assumes that voters reward or punish politicians for observed welfare, but public services are often delivered by non-state actors. When citizens can see benefits but not who provided them, politicians might be able to redirect state resources with little electoral cost. I argue that external provision can weaken democratic accountability by relaxing the electoral constraint that disciplines the use of public resources. I provide experimental evidence from the Philippines showing that a community-driven development program led politicians to reduce public service spending and direct procurement to political supporters. These effects are concentrated in municipalities with low political competition, where incumbents face weaker electoral pressure. I offer suggestive evidence that these political consequences attenuated the program’s welfare gains. These results identify a limitation of democratic accountability: sharing service delivery between state and non-state actors can weaken constraints on both the level and distribution of public resources.
